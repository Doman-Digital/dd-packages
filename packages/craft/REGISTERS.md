# Writing in a register

**Canonical.** How to write in a chosen register, and how to check where a
draft sits. `COPY.md` says what every piece of house copy avoids; a register
says what a draft reaches for.

| Register | For |
| --- | --- |
| `plain` | forms, policies, instructions, professional services |
| `persuasive` | landing pages, service pages, adverts, sales emails |
| `warm` | customer emails, enquiry replies, newsletters |
| `literary` | premium brands, makers, hospitality |

## Using it

1. **Before drafting**, read the brief: `craft register brief warm`. Paste it
   into a prompt if a model is drafting.
2. **After drafting**, run `craft copy <file>` as always. The blocking tier
   and the proof rule hold in every register.
3. **For a document of five paragraphs or more**, run
   `craft register check <file> --as warm`. It lists the style features that
   fall outside the usual range for that register, and which human baseline
   the draft reads closest to. It never fails a run.

## What the check can and cannot do

It measures style, not content: sentence length, commas, clauses,
nominalisations, pronoun openers, paired phrases, contractions, person and
passive voice. Names, figures and specifics are left out, because they say
what a text is about, not how it is written.

It needs five paragraphs of two to eight sentences each. In
`calibration/copy-shape/report-registers.md`, single passages from different
registers overlap too much to place (26 of 258 pair tests separate), while
five-paragraph averages separate every register pair (78 of 258). Anything
shorter gets the brief, not a measurement.

Each range is the middle 80% of human documents, so one in five human
documents falls outside any single range. Read the findings as a
read-through. They are not a grade.

Each brief is compared with the nearest free human baseline, and none is
exact:

| Brief | Baseline | Not quite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `plain` | Hansard Commons, 2010 to 2019 | edited debate, not plain writing |
| `persuasive` | UK small-business websites before 2021 | 715 passages only |
| `warm` | Enron sent email, 1999 to 2002 | corporate and American |
| `literary` | English fiction and essays, 1726 to 1925 | older than any brand |

The bands in `src/register/profile.ts` are generated by
`node scripts/shape/registers.mjs --profile`. Rerun it after adding a
baseline; never edit the file by hand.

## Plain and trustworthy (`plain`)

**Use for:** Forms, policies, terms, instructions, and professional services where the reader must act correctly.

**Before you draft:**

1. Put the most important information first, then taper down to the detail. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
2. Split any sentence over 25 words into two. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
3. Choose the short, common word: buy, help, about. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
4. Write in the active voice, so the reader sees who does what. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
5. Address the reader as "you", as one person you can actually help. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
6. Use positive contractions such as "you'll", and write negatives out in full: "do not", "cannot". *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
7. Write headings that say what the section tells the reader, starting with a verb where you can. *(GOV.UK writing guidelines)*
8. Give each paragraph one job. *(house judgement)*

**Example (fictional):**

- Before: Should you wish to apply for a residents' parking permit, an application form must be completed and submitted to the council prior to the commencement of the permit period.
- After: Apply for a residents' parking permit before you need it. Fill in the form online. It takes about 10 minutes.
- Why: The action comes first, the council is no longer the subject, and each sentence does one thing.

**Sources:**

- [GOV.UK writing guidelines](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/): Clear language (the 25-word split, short words, active voice, contractions), clear structure (most important first, headings) and right tone (address the user as "you"). Checked against the live pages on 2026-09-30.

## Persuasive and concise (`persuasive`)

**Use for:** Landing pages, service pages, adverts and sales emails: copy whose job is one action.

**Before you draft:**

1. Put the promise in the headline: what the reader gets, in words they would use. *(Ogilvy, Ogilvy on Advertising (1983))*
2. Back each claim with something the reader can check: a number, a name, a date, a price. *(COPY.md)*
3. Write to one reader, as "you", about their problem before your service. *(Caples, Tested Advertising Methods)*
4. Name the mechanism: how it works, how long it takes, what it costs. *(COPY.md)*
5. Answer the objection the reader already has, with evidence, in the sentence that follows the claim. *(house judgement)*
6. Let the copy run as long as the facts last, and cut each sentence that carries none. *(Ogilvy, Ogilvy on Advertising (1983))*
7. Close with one specific action and what happens straight after it. *(COPY.md)*

**Example (fictional):**

- Before: Transform your garden with our premium landscaping solutions. Quality you can trust.
- After: We lay a 40 square metre Indian sandstone patio in three days, for £4,800 fitted. Every job since 2014 is on our reviews page. Book a free site visit and get a fixed quote within 48 hours.
- Why: Size, material, time and price replace the adjectives, the proof sits next to the claim, and the close says what happens next. Fictional business.

**Sources:**

- Ogilvy, Ogilvy on Advertising (1983): Headlines carry the promise because far more people read the headline than the body; specific, informative copy outsells vague copy. Taken from the widely quoted principles; a page-by-page check against the book is still to do.
- Caples, Tested Advertising Methods: Headlines that appeal to the reader's self-interest, and copy judged by tested response rather than taste. A page-by-page check against the book is still to do.
- COPY.md: Writing directives (lead with the fact, name the mechanism, persuade with something checkable) and the CTA rule.

## Warm and conversational (`warm`)

**Use for:** Customer emails, replies to enquiries, newsletters and small-business updates.

**Before you draft:**

1. Write the way you would talk to a customer you like: contractions, everyday words. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
2. Keep words and sentences simple; being clear matters more than being entertaining. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
3. Say what the reader can do, in positive language. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
4. Use the active voice. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
5. Match the tone to how the reader feels right now: lighter for good news, calm and direct for a problem. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
6. Keep humour dry and occasional, and only where it comes naturally. *(Mailchimp Content Style Guide)*
7. Sign off as a person, with a name. *(house judgement)*

**Example (fictional):**

- Before: Dear Valued Customer, We wish to inform you that your order has been dispatched and will be delivered in due course.
- After: Hi Sam, your order's on its way. It left our Leicester workshop this morning and should reach you on Thursday. Any questions, just reply to this email. Priya
- Why: A name at each end, a real day and place instead of "in due course", and a next step. Fictional business.

**Sources:**

- [Mailchimp Content Style Guide](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/): Voice and tone (plainspoken, genuine, dry humour; clear before entertaining; adjust tone to the reader's state) and grammar and mechanics (active voice, contractions, positive language). Its allowance for emoji does not apply: emoji block under COPY.md. Checked against the live pages on 2026-09-30.

## Elegant and literary (`literary`)

**Use for:** Premium brands, makers, hospitality and anything sold on craft and taste rather than price.

**Before you draft:**

1. Choose one precise noun over two adjectives. *(house judgement)*
2. Let sentence length follow the thought: a long sentence to build, a short one to land. *(COPY.md)*
3. Carry the detail in concrete facts: material, place, process, time. *(house judgement)*
4. Trust the reader with one image, state it once, and move on. *(house judgement)*
5. Keep claims quiet and exact, and let the facts carry the prestige. *(house judgement)*
6. Draw any metaphor from the maker's own work, and hold it for the whole piece. *(house judgement)*
7. Read the draft aloud and rework each line that trips the breath. *(house judgement)*

**Example (fictional):**

- Before: Our luxurious, handcrafted candles create an unforgettable ambience of elegance and sophistication.
- After: Each candle is poured by hand in our Hebden Bridge workshop, forty to a batch. The wax is English rapeseed and the scent is cedar, cut with a little smoke. It burns for sixty hours, and the glass is yours to keep.
- Why: Place, material, scent and burn time do the work the adjectives claimed. Fictional business.

**Sources:**

- house judgement: No public style authority exists for premium-brand prose. These directives are house judgement, to be revised as the estate grows.
- [Register separation, 2026-09-29](calibration/copy-shape/report-registers.md): Literary prose (Gutenberg) carries about twice the comma density of the other human registers, the one feature that separated it from all four. Longer, jointed sentences are normal here.

**Always:** Every register keeps the COPY.md blocking tier and the proof rule. Draft first, then run `craft copy <file>` on the draft.
